Hadean Eon

4.6 – 4.0 billion years ago

Earth's Hellish Beginning

Named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld, the Hadean Eon represents the most extreme chapter of Earth's history. For nearly 600 million years, our planet was an almost unrecognizable world of molten rock, constant bombardment, and toxic atmospheres. No rocks survive from the earliest Hadean — the evidence has been recycled back into Earth's interior — making this eon one of the most mysterious periods in planetary science.

The Magma Ocean

Immediately after its formation, Earth's surface was covered by a global magma ocean — a sea of molten silicate rock hundreds of kilometers deep. Heat from three sources maintained this infernal state: the kinetic energy of accretion (constant impacts), gravitational energy released as dense iron sank to form the core, and the decay of short-lived radioactive isotopes like aluminium-26 and iron-60.

Surface temperatures reached 2,000–3,000 K, hot enough to glow a dull red-orange. The sky above was a thick, opaque blanket of rock vapor and steam. As the magma ocean slowly cooled over millions of years, the first ultramafic crust began to solidify, only to be repeatedly shattered and remelted by incoming impacts.

The First Atmosphere

Earth's earliest atmosphere bore no resemblance to the one we breathe today. Intense volcanic outgassing released enormous volumes of water vapor, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, sulfur dioxide, and hydrogen chloride. The atmospheric pressure may have been 100 times greater than today's, creating a crushing, superheated greenhouse.

There was essentially no free oxygen. Any hydrogen released was light enough to escape to space, while heavier gases accumulated. The intense greenhouse effect kept surface temperatures elevated even as the Sun was about 30% dimmer than it is today — a condition known as the Faint Young Sun Paradox.

The First Oceans

As Earth cooled below the boiling point of water (accounting for the extreme atmospheric pressure), the vast quantities of water vapor in the atmosphere began to condense. Torrential rains — lasting perhaps millions of years — gradually filled the first ocean basins. By approximately 4.4 billion years ago, evidence from ancient zircon crystals suggests that liquid water was present on Earth's surface, far earlier than once believed.

These primordial oceans were nothing like modern seas. They were likely acidic, rich in dissolved iron, and heated by volcanic vents. Yet their very existence created the conditions for the chemistry that would eventually give rise to life.

The Missing Record

Almost no rocks survive from the Hadean. Earth's active geology — plate tectonics, erosion, and metamorphism — has recycled virtually all of the original crust back into the mantle. Our knowledge of this eon comes from:

  • Jack Hills Zircons — Tiny, nearly indestructible zircon crystals found in younger sedimentary rocks in Western Australia, dated to 4.4 billion years old. They are the oldest known pieces of Earth.
  • Lunar Geology — The Moon preserves a record of the early solar system that Earth has lost, including impact craters from the Late Heavy Bombardment.
  • Meteorites — Primitive meteorites record conditions in the early solar system and help constrain models of Earth's formation.
  • Computer Models — Sophisticated simulations of planetary accretion, magma ocean cooling, and atmospheric evolution fill in the gaps where physical evidence is absent.

Did You Know?

The Hadean Eon lasted longer than the entire time from the extinction of the dinosaurs to the present day — roughly 600 million years. Yet we have fewer surviving rocks from this entire eon than from a single modern volcanic eruption.
