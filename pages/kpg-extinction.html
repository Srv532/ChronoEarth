The K-Pg Extinction

~66 million years ago

The Day the Dinosaurs Died

Sixty-six million years ago, a 10–15 km wide asteroid struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico at roughly 20 km/s (72,000 km/h), releasing energy equivalent to 10 billion Hiroshima bombs. The Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) extinction — formerly called the K-T extinction — ended the 165-million-year reign of the dinosaurs and eliminated approximately 76% of all species on Earth. It is the most famous mass extinction in history and one of the best-understood, thanks to a remarkable convergence of geological evidence.

The Chicxulub Impact

The impactor struck the shallow seas of the Yucatán Peninsula, creating the Chicxulub crater — a buried impact structure approximately 180 km in diameter and 20 km deep. The impact had immediate and cascading effects:

  1. The First Seconds — The asteroid punched through the atmosphere in about one second. Upon impact, it vaporized instantly along with cubic kilometers of limestone, gypsum, and seawater. A fireball larger than the Sun's diameter briefly appeared, incinerating everything within 1,500 km.
  2. The First Minutes — A shockwave flattened forests for thousands of kilometers. Molten ejecta — billions of tonnes of superheated rock droplets — were launched on ballistic trajectories around the globe, re-entering the atmosphere and heating the sky to oven-like temperatures. Forests worldwide ignited.
  3. The First Hours — Magnitude 10+ earthquakes — the strongest ever to shake the planet — radiated outward. Megatsunamis up to 1.5 km high surged across the Gulf of Mexico and into the Atlantic and Pacific.
  4. The First Months — Soot from global wildfires, sulfate aerosols from vaporized gypsum, and pulverized rock dust blocked sunlight for months to years. Global temperatures plummeted. Photosynthesis collapsed, shutting down the food chain from the bottom up.
  5. The First Years — Acid rain from sulfuric and nitric acids devastated terrestrial vegetation and acidified surface ocean waters. The ozone layer was severely damaged.
  6. The Following Centuries — As dust cleared, massive CO₂ released from vaporized carbonates drove intense greenhouse warming. The climate whiplashed from "impact winter" to extreme warmth.

The Evidence

  • The Iridium Layer — In 1980, Luis and Walter Alvarez discovered a thin clay layer at the K-Pg boundary enriched in iridium — an element rare on Earth's surface but abundant in asteroids. This layer has now been found at over 350 sites worldwide.
  • Shocked Quartz — Quartz grains with distinctive parallel deformation lamellae, produced only by extreme pressures from hypervelocity impacts or nuclear explosions, are found globally in K-Pg boundary sediments.
  • Tektites and Spherules — Tiny glass beads (tektites) and spherules formed from molten ejecta are found in K-Pg sediments, particularly thick near the Gulf of Mexico.
  • The Chicxulub Crater — Discovered in 1991 through gravity anomaly mapping, the buried crater on the Yucatán Peninsula is precisely the right age (66.043 ± 0.011 Ma) and size to explain the K-Pg extinction.
  • Tanis Fossil Site — Discovered in North Dakota, this site preserves organisms killed within minutes to hours of the impact, including fish with tektites lodged in their gills.

The Deccan Traps: A Complicating Factor

The Chicxulub impact coincided with massive volcanic eruptions of the Deccan Traps in what is now India — one of the largest volcanic events in the last 500 million years. The Deccan eruptions released enormous volumes of CO₂ and SO₂ over approximately 750,000 years, stressing ecosystems before, during, and after the impact. Most researchers now view the extinction as primarily impact-driven, but the Deccan volcanism likely weakened ecosystems beforehand and hindered recovery afterward.

What Survived?

The extinction was selective. Organisms that survived tended to share certain characteristics:

  • Small body size — No land animal larger than about 25 kg survived.
  • Ability to shelter — Burrowing mammals, freshwater turtles, and crocodilians persisted.
  • Detritus-based food chains — Organisms that fed on dead organic matter (fungi, insects, small mammals) were less affected than those dependent on photosynthesis.
  • Seed-bearing plants — Seeds could remain dormant through the "impact winter" and germinate when sunlight returned.
  • Birds — A single lineage of small, ground-dwelling or near-ground-dwelling dinosaurs survived and gave rise to all 10,000+ species of modern birds.

Did You Know?

The Chicxulub asteroid hit at the worst possible angle (~60° from horizontal) and in the worst possible location — a shallow sea overlying sulfur-rich gypsum deposits. Had it struck deep ocean basalt instead, far less sulfate would have been vaporized, and the extinction might have been significantly less severe. The dinosaurs may have been victims of extraordinarily bad luck.
