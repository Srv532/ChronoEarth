Snowball Earth

~720 – 635 million years ago

When the Planet Froze

At least twice during the late Proterozoic, Earth experienced glaciations so extreme that ice sheets may have extended from the poles all the way to the equator, encasing the entire planet in a shell of ice up to a kilometer thick. These "Snowball Earth" events represent the most severe climate episodes in Earth's history — and, paradoxically, may have been a crucial catalyst for the evolution of complex animal life.

The Two Great Glaciations

Sturtian Glaciation (~717 – 660 Ma)

The Sturtian was the longer and possibly more severe of the two events, lasting roughly 57 million years. Glacial deposits from this period have been found on every continent, including locations that were at tropical latitudes at the time. Global average temperatures may have plummeted to −50°C, and the oceans were sealed beneath hundreds of meters of ice.

Marinoan Glaciation (~650 – 635 Ma)

The Marinoan glaciation was shorter but equally dramatic. It ended abruptly around 635 million years ago and was immediately followed by the deposition of distinctive "cap carbonates" — thick layers of limestone and dolomite that blanketed the glacial deposits worldwide. These cap carbonates are the chemical fingerprint of a rapid greenhouse-driven meltdown.

What Caused the Freeze?

  • Rodinia's Breakup — The supercontinent Rodinia straddled the equator. As it fragmented, vast expanses of fresh continental rock were exposed to tropical rainfall. Chemical weathering of silicate rocks consumes atmospheric CO₂, and the enhanced weathering may have drawn greenhouse gas levels low enough to trigger runaway cooling.
  • Ice-Albedo Feedback — Once ice sheets advanced far enough, the high reflectivity (albedo) of ice and snow reflected more sunlight back to space, cooling the planet further, which grew more ice — a positive feedback loop that drove the glaciation to completion.
  • Low Solar Luminosity — The Sun was about 6% dimmer than today, providing less warmth and making the planet more susceptible to glaciation.

How Did Earth Escape?

With the oceans sealed under ice, the water cycle effectively shut down — weathering of rocks ceased, and the primary mechanism for removing CO₂ from the atmosphere was eliminated. However, volcanic eruptions continued uninterrupted beneath the ice, pumping CO₂ into the atmosphere for millions of years.

Over tens of millions of years, atmospheric CO₂ concentrations may have risen to 350 times modern levels — roughly 13% of the atmosphere. Eventually, this extreme greenhouse effect overwhelmed the ice-albedo feedback, triggering a catastrophic melt. The transition from icehouse to greenhouse may have occurred within just a few thousand years, producing some of the most extreme climate swings Earth has ever experienced.

Life Under the Ice

How did life survive? Several refugia likely sustained biological communities through the Snowball:

  • Open-water areas near volcanic hotspots and thin tropical ice where sunlight could penetrate
  • Hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor, powered by Earth's internal heat
  • Cryoconite holes — small melt pools on glacial surfaces that host microbial ecosystems even in modern glaciers
  • Subglacial lakes — liquid water trapped beneath thick ice sheets, similar to Antarctica's Lake Vostok today

The Aftermath: Gateway to Complex Life

The post-Snowball world was transformed. The extreme climate swings drove a massive influx of nutrients into the oceans from continental weathering. Atmospheric oxygen levels surged — perhaps reaching near-modern levels for the first time. Within 100 million years of the Marinoan's end, the Ediacaran biota appeared: the first large, multicellular organisms. And just 94 million years after that, the Cambrian Explosion would fill the oceans with complex animal life.

Many scientists believe the Snowball Earth events were not a catastrophe for evolution but a crucible — the environmental stress that pushed life toward greater complexity.

Did You Know?

During Snowball Earth, the ice covering the oceans may have been so thick that the pressure at its base kept the water liquid even at temperatures below normal freezing. The ice surface would have been one of the most desolate landscapes imaginable — a white, windswept desert stretching from pole to pole.
