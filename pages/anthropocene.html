The Anthropocene

~1950 CE – Present (proposed)

The Human Epoch

The Anthropocene is a proposed geological epoch defined by the pervasive and lasting impact of human activity on Earth's geology, climate, and ecosystems. While not yet formally ratified by the International Commission on Stratigraphy, the term has gained wide scientific and public usage because the evidence is overwhelming: humans have become a geological force, altering the planet at a pace and scale unprecedented in 4.5 billion years of Earth history.

Geological Markers

Future geologists examining rock layers millions of years from now would find a sharp boundary marking the onset of the Anthropocene. The proposed start date of ~1950 is based on the appearance of distinctive markers in the geological record:

  • Radioactive Isotopes — Nuclear weapons testing in the 1950s–60s deposited plutonium-239 and carbon-14 in sediments worldwide, creating a globally synchronous stratigraphic marker.
  • Plastics — Microplastics are now found in sediments from deep ocean trenches to mountain glaciers. Plastic production began at scale in the 1950s and will leave a durable stratigraphic signal.
  • Fly Ash — Spherical carbonaceous particles from fossil fuel combustion are now ubiquitous in sediments worldwide.
  • Concrete — Humans have produced enough concrete to cover the entire surface of the Earth in a layer 2 mm thick. Concrete fragments will persist in the geological record as "technofossils."
  • Nitrogen and Phosphorus Spikes — The Haber-Bosch process (industrial nitrogen fixation) and phosphate mining have doubled the global flux of nitrogen and tripled phosphorus, leaving chemical signatures in lake sediments, ice cores, and soils.
  • Chicken Bones — The domestic chicken is the most numerous bird on Earth (~33 billion at any time). Broiler chicken bones — morphologically distinct from all wild species — will be one of the most common vertebrate fossils of this era.

Climate Change

Since the Industrial Revolution, burning fossil fuels has released over 2.4 trillion tonnes of CO₂ into the atmosphere, raising atmospheric CO₂ from a pre-industrial ~280 ppm to over 420 ppm — the highest level in at least 3 million years. The rate of change is roughly 10 times faster than any natural CO₂ increase in the geological record, including the PETM.

Consequences already observed include:

  • Global average temperature has risen by ~1.2°C since 1850
  • Arctic sea ice has declined by ~40% since satellite monitoring began (1979)
  • Global sea level has risen ~20 cm since 1900, accelerating each decade
  • Ocean pH has decreased by 0.1 units (a 26% increase in acidity)
  • The frequency and intensity of extreme heat events has increased significantly

The Sixth Mass Extinction

Many scientists argue that Earth is in the early stages of a sixth mass extinction driven by human activity. Current species extinction rates are estimated to be 100 to 1,000 times higher than background (natural) rates.

  • Habitat Destruction — Roughly 75% of Earth's ice-free land surface has been significantly altered by human activity. Tropical deforestation — particularly in the Amazon, Southeast Asia, and Central Africa — eliminates the most species-rich habitats on Earth.
  • Megafauna Loss — Most large mammals outside Africa have been driven extinct since humans arrived on each continent. Mammoths, giant sloths, diprotodons, and moa all disappeared within centuries of human colonization.
  • Insect Decline — Studies suggest insect biomass has declined by 75% in some regions over the past 30 years, driven by pesticide use, habitat loss, and climate change.
  • Ocean Collapse — Overfishing has reduced large predatory fish populations by ~90% since 1950. Coral reef bleaching events are becoming more frequent and severe.

The Great Acceleration

Since 1950, virtually every indicator of human impact on Earth has shown exponential growth — a pattern scientists call "The Great Acceleration." Human population tripled (from 2.5 to 8 billion). Energy use increased fivefold. Fertilizer use increased eightfold. The number of motor vehicles grew from 70 million to 1.4 billion. International tourism increased forty-fold. This acceleration has no precedent in the geological record.

Deep Time Perspective

From the viewpoint of Earth's 4.5-billion-year history, the Anthropocene is a blink — barely an instant. Yet its impact may rival the great geological events of the past. The current rate of carbon release exceeds the Siberian Traps eruptions that caused the Permian extinction. The rate of species loss may approach that of the K-Pg asteroid impact. Whether the Anthropocene becomes a brief perturbation or a permanent scar on Earth's geological record depends entirely on the choices made by the species that named it.

Did You Know?

The total mass of all human-made objects — buildings, roads, machines, plastics, clothing — now exceeds the total mass of all living biomass on Earth. As of 2020, the "technosphere" weighs approximately 1.1 trillion tonnes, compared to ~1.0 trillion tonnes for all living organisms combined.
