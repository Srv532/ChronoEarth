Human Evolution

~7 million years ago – Present

The Hominin Journey

The story of human evolution is not a straight line from ape to human but a sprawling, branching bush. Over the past 7 million years, at least 20 distinct hominin species have walked the Earth — most of them now extinct. Only one lineage survives: Homo sapiens. Our evolutionary journey took us from the forests of Africa to every habitable continent, transforming us from small, tree-dwelling apes into the most ecologically dominant species the planet has ever seen.

The Earliest Hominins

  • Sahelanthropus tchadensis (~7 Ma) — Discovered in Chad in 2001, this is one of the oldest known potential hominins. Its skull shows a mix of ape-like and human-like features, including a forward- positioned foramen magnum suggesting upright posture.
  • Ardipithecus ramidus (~4.4 Ma) — "Ardi," discovered in Ethiopia, was a woodland-dwelling hominin that could walk upright on the ground but also climbed trees. Her anatomy challenges the assumption that bipedalism evolved on open savannahs.

Australopithecus: The Southern Apes

Australopithecus species were the dominant hominins from roughly 4 to 2 million years ago, living across eastern and southern Africa.

  • Australopithecus afarensis (~3.9 – 2.9 Ma) — The species of "Lucy," one of the most famous fossils ever found. Discovered in Ethiopia in 1974 by Donald Johanson, Lucy was a small (1.1 m tall), bipedal female with a brain about one-third the size of a modern human's. The Laetoli footprints in Tanzania (3.6 Ma) — preserved in volcanic ash — confirm that her species walked fully upright.
  • Australopithecus africanus (~3.3 – 2.1 Ma) — The "Taung Child," discovered in South Africa in 1924 by Raymond Dart, was the first australopith ever identified. It had a slightly larger brain and more gracile features than A. afarensis.
  • Australopithecus sediba (~2.0 Ma) — Found in South Africa in 2008, this species shows a mosaic of australopith and Homo-like features, making it a candidate ancestor for our genus.

The Genus Homo

Homo habilis (~2.4 – 1.4 Ma)

"Handy Man" — the first species assigned to our genus. H. habilis had a brain roughly 50% larger than australopiths and is associated with the Oldowan stone tool industry — simple but effective cobble tools used to cut meat and crack bones for marrow. Tool use may have enabled access to higher-quality food, driving brain growth.

Homo erectus (~1.9 Ma – ~110 Ka)

Perhaps the most successful hominin species before sapiens. H. erectus was the first hominin to leave Africa, spreading to the Middle East, Asia, and Indonesia. It had a modern body plan — tall, long-legged, narrow-hipped — adapted for endurance running and long-distance walking. H. erectus mastered fire (by ~1 Ma), developed the symmetrical Acheulean hand axe, and may have built simple shelters. With a brain size of 900–1100 cm³, it approached the lower range of modern humans.

Homo heidelbergensis (~700 – 200 Ka)

A tall, robust species with a brain nearly as large as ours (~1,200 cm³). H. heidelbergensis is likely the common ancestor of both Neanderthals and modern humans. It hunted large game with wooden spears, built complex shelters, and may have been the first hominin to intentionally bury its dead.

Homo neanderthalensis (~400 – 40 Ka)

Neanderthals were our closest evolutionary relatives — stocky, cold-adapted humans who thrived in Ice Age Europe and western Asia. They had brains slightly larger than ours on average (~1,500 cm³), made sophisticated Mousterian stone tools, used fire expertly, buried their dead (sometimes with grave goods), created art (cave paintings in Spain), wore eagle-talon jewelry, and used medicinal plants. When Homo sapiens expanded into Europe around 45,000 years ago, the two species interbred. Today, most non-African humans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA.

Homo sapiens (~300 Ka – Present)

Our species evolved in Africa, with the oldest known fossils coming from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (~300 Ka). By 70,000 years ago, H. sapiens began dispersing out of Africa, eventually reaching every continent except Antarctica. What set us apart was not necessarily brain size but the capacity for symbolic thought, language, and cumulative culture — the ability to build knowledge across generations. Art (cave paintings at Chauvet, ~36 Ka), musical instruments (bone flutes, ~40 Ka), and sophisticated tools appeared increasingly from 100,000 years ago onward.

Key Adaptations

  • Bipedalism — Walking upright freed the hands for tool use and carrying, improved energy efficiency for long-distance travel, and raised the head above grassland vegetation for better predator detection.
  • Enlarged Brain — Human brain size tripled over 3 million years (from ~400 cm³ to ~1,400 cm³), fueled by high-quality diets (meat, cooked food) and social complexity. The brain consumes 20% of our energy despite being only 2% of body mass.
  • Fire and Cooking — Controlled use of fire (by ~1 Ma) provided warmth, protection, light, and — crucially — cooked food. Cooking breaks down tough plant fibers and proteins, extracting more calories and enabling gut reduction while supporting brain growth.
  • Language — The evolution of complex language enabled teaching, planning, storytelling, and the transmission of culture across generations. It is arguably the single most important adaptation in human evolution.

Did You Know?

All living humans are remarkably genetically similar. The entire human species has less genetic diversity than a single troop of chimpanzees. This is because our species likely went through a severe population bottleneck — perhaps as few as 10,000 breeding individuals — around 70,000 years ago, possibly linked to the eruption of the Toba supervolcano in Indonesia.
