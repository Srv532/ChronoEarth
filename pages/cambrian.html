The Cambrian Explosion

~541 – 485 million years ago

Life's Big Bang

The Cambrian Explosion is one of the most dramatic events in the history of life. In a geologically brief window of roughly 20–25 million years at the start of the Cambrian Period, virtually all major animal body plans (phyla) appeared in the fossil record for the first time. Before the Cambrian, the oceans held soft-bodied Ediacaran organisms and microbial mats. After it, the seas teemed with arthropods, mollusks, chordates, echinoderms, brachiopods, and worms of bewildering variety.

What Appeared?

The Cambrian saw the first appearance of:

  • Hard shells and skeletons — Biomineralization (the ability to build shells from calcium carbonate, silica, or calcium phosphate) evolved independently in many lineages, dramatically improving the fossil record.
  • Eyes — The compound eyes of trilobites were among the first sophisticated visual systems. Vision may have accelerated an evolutionary "arms race" between predators and prey.
  • Predation — Anomalocaris, reaching up to 1 meter long, was the first large active predator, fundamentally changing ecological dynamics.
  • Burrowing — Animals began burrowing into the seafloor for the first time (the "Agronomic Revolution"), mixing sediment layers and transforming ocean chemistry.

The Burgess Shale

Discovered in 1909 by Charles Doolittle Walcott in the Canadian Rockies of British Columbia, the Burgess Shale is one of the most important fossil deposits ever found. Dated to approximately 508 million years ago, its exceptional preservation captures soft-bodied organisms that are normally lost to decay, providing an unparalleled window into Cambrian marine ecosystems.

Notable Burgess Shale Organisms

  • Anomalocaris — The "abnormal shrimp," a meter-long apex predator with grasping appendages and circular, tooth-lined mouth. The largest animal of its time.
  • Hallucigenia — A bizarre worm-like creature with spines on its back and pairs of legs. Originally reconstructed upside-down, its correct orientation was only determined decades later.
  • Opabinia — A five-eyed arthropod with a flexible forward-facing proboscis tipped with a claw. When first presented at a scientific conference, the audience reportedly burst into laughter at its strangeness.
  • Pikaia — A small, leaf-shaped swimmer considered one of the earliest known chordates — the group that would eventually give rise to all vertebrates, including humans.
  • Wiwaxia — An armored, slug-like creature covered in overlapping scales and defensive spines, whose exact evolutionary relationships remain debated.

What Triggered the Explosion?

No single cause explains the Cambrian Explosion. Instead, multiple factors likely converged:

  • Rising Oxygen — Atmospheric and oceanic oxygen levels increased sharply in the late Proterozoic, crossing thresholds needed to support larger, more active organisms with higher metabolic demands.
  • Genetic Toolkit — Hox genes and other developmental "master switches" that control body plan formation had evolved in the Precambrian, providing the genetic infrastructure for rapid morphological innovation.
  • Ecological Arms Race — The evolution of predation created intense selective pressure for defensive innovations (shells, spines, burrowing, speed), driving rapid diversification.
  • Post-Snowball Conditions — The aftermath of Snowball Earth created nutrient-rich oceans and new ecological opportunities, opening vast empty niches for colonization.

Trilobites: Icons of the Cambrian

No group epitomizes the Cambrian more than trilobites. These marine arthropods were among the first animals with complex, compound eyes — each eye contained hundreds of individually oriented calcite crystal lenses. Trilobites diversified into over 20,000 species across 300 million years and are found on every continent. Their calcite exoskeletons made them excellent fossils, and they serve as critical index fossils for dating Paleozoic rocks.

Did You Know?

Trilobite eyes had lenses made of single crystals of calcite — the only known organism to use a mineral lens. Each crystal was oriented to eliminate double refraction (birefringence), a solution so elegant that it was independently described by the physicist Huygens in the 17th century.
