Cenozoic Era

66 million years ago – Present

The Age of Mammals

The Cenozoic Era — meaning "new life" — is the current geological era and the chapter in which mammals, birds, and flowering plants rose to dominance. Beginning in the aftermath of the K-Pg extinction, the Cenozoic has been characterized by a long-term global cooling trend, the formation of modern continental configurations, the evolution of grasslands, and ultimately the emergence of our own species. Though it spans "only" 66 million years (less than 1.5% of Earth's history), it is the era we know in greatest detail.

Paleogene Period (66 – 23 Ma)

Paleocene Epoch (66 – 56 Ma)

In the immediate aftermath of the asteroid impact, small mammals inherited a devastated world. With large dinosaurs gone, mammals diversified rapidly into ecological niches previously closed to them. Within 10 million years, the first large land mammals appeared, along with early primates, rodents, and hoofed herbivores. Forests recovered and expanded, initially dominated by ferns (the "fern spike"), then gradually replaced by flowering plants.

Eocene Epoch (56 – 34 Ma)

The Early Eocene was the warmest period of the entire Cenozoic. During the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma), global temperatures spiked by 5–8°C over a few thousand years, likely driven by massive carbon release from volcanic activity or methane hydrate destabilization. Palm trees grew in Alaska, crocodilians lived above the Arctic Circle, and Antarctica was forested. Modern mammal orders — including whales, bats, elephants, horses, and primates — all appeared during the Eocene.

Oligocene Epoch (34 – 23 Ma)

Global cooling intensified during the Oligocene as Antarctica separated from South America, allowing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current to form. This current thermally isolated Antarctica, and the first permanent Antarctic ice sheet appeared around 34 Ma — a pivotal moment in global climate history. Open woodlands and early grasslands began replacing dense tropical forests in many regions, driving changes in mammalian evolution toward more open-habitat species.

Neogene Period (23 – 2.6 Ma)

Miocene Epoch (23 – 5.3 Ma)

The Miocene saw the dramatic expansion of grasslands — a new biome that transformed terrestrial ecosystems. Grasses evolved silica-rich leaves (phytoliths) that wore down teeth, driving the evolution of high-crowned (hypsodont) teeth in horses, antelopes, and other grazers. Large predators included saber-toothed cats, bear-dogs (amphicyonids), and the giant "Hell Pig" Daeodon. The first hominins — the lineage leading to humans — diverged from other great apes in Africa during the Late Miocene.

Pliocene Epoch (5.3 – 2.6 Ma)

The Pliocene witnessed continued cooling and the closure of the Central American Seaway as the Isthmus of Panama formed (~3 Ma), connecting North and South America. This triggered the Great American Biotic Interchange — a massive migration of animals between the two continents. Placental predators from the north (cats, dogs, bears) invaded the south, while armadillos, opossums, and giant ground sloths moved north. The Northern Hemisphere's first major ice sheets began forming.

Quaternary Period (2.6 Ma – Present)

Pleistocene Epoch (2.6 Ma – 11,700 years ago)

The Pleistocene is the Ice Age epoch — characterized by repeated glacial- interglacial cycles driven by Milankovitch orbital variations. At glacial maxima, ice sheets up to 3 km thick covered much of North America and Northern Europe. Sea levels dropped by up to 120 meters, exposing land bridges (including Beringia, connecting Asia and North America). The Pleistocene was the age of megafauna: woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats (Smilodon), giant ground sloths (Megatherium), and cave bears. The genus Homo evolved and diversified during this epoch, with Homo sapiens appearing roughly 300,000 years ago.

Holocene Epoch (11,700 years ago – Present)

The Holocene is the current interglacial period, characterized by relatively warm, stable climate. It encompasses the entirety of human civilization — agriculture, writing, urbanization, science, and technology all arose within this brief warm window. Whether the Holocene is truly a distinct epoch or merely the latest interglacial within an ongoing ice age remains debated.

Key Cenozoic Trends

  • Global Cooling — A 50-million-year cooling trend from the hothouse Eocene to the ice ages of the Pleistocene, driven by falling CO₂, ocean circulation changes, and mountain building (Himalayas, Andes).
  • Grassland Expansion — Grasslands replaced forests across much of the mid-latitudes, transforming mammalian evolution and ecology.
  • Continental Positions — India collided with Asia (~50 Ma), raising the Himalayas and Tibetan Plateau. Australia separated from Antarctica and drifted north. Africa approached and partially merged with Europe.
  • Mammalian Gigantism — Free from dinosaur competition, mammals evolved to enormous sizes: Paraceratherium (the largest land mammal, 5 meters at the shoulder), blue whales (the largest animal ever to live), and elephants.

Did You Know?

The blue whale, alive today, is the largest animal ever known to have existed — larger than any dinosaur. At up to 30 meters long and 190 tonnes, it dwarfs even the largest sauropods. Its heart is the size of a small car, and its aorta is wide enough for a human child to crawl through.
