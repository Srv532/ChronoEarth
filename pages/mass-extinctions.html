The Big Five Mass Extinctions

A cross-cutting theme spanning 445 – 66 million years ago

When Life Nearly Ended

Over the past 500 million years, Earth has experienced five catastrophic mass extinction events — episodes in which a significant proportion of all species on Earth were wiped out in geologically brief intervals. Each extinction reshaped the trajectory of life, eliminating dominant groups and creating opportunities for previously marginal lineages to rise. Understanding these events is not just academic — their causes (volcanic climate change, ocean anoxia, asteroid impacts) offer sobering lessons for the present.

1. End-Ordovician Extinction (~445 Ma)

Species Lost ~85% of marine species
Primary Cause Glaciation and sea level change
Duration Two pulses over ~1 million years

As the supercontinent Gondwana drifted over the South Pole, massive ice sheets formed, locking up water and dropping sea levels by up to 100 meters. The shallow continental shelves where most marine life thrived were drained. A second pulse occurred as glaciers melted, flooding shelves with cold, oxygen-poor deep water. Brachiopods, bryozoans, graptolites, and trilobites were devastated. Notable survivors included early jawless fish and some cephalopods.

2. Late Devonian Extinction (~375 – 359 Ma)

Species Lost ~75% of species
Primary Cause Ocean anoxia, possibly linked to forest expansion
Duration Multiple pulses over ~20 million years

The Late Devonian extinction was not a single event but a protracted crisis with at least two major pulses: the Kellwasser Event (~372 Ma) and the Hangenberg Event (~359 Ma). Tropical reef ecosystems were nearly obliterated — reef-building organisms were so thoroughly eliminated that complex reefs did not fully recover for 100 million years. The spread of the first large forests may have contributed by accelerating soil formation and nutrient runoff, causing algal blooms and ocean dead zones. Placoderms went extinct entirely.

3. End-Permian Extinction (~252 Ma)

Species Lost ~96% of marine species, ~70% of terrestrial vertebrates
Primary Cause Siberian Traps volcanism
Duration ~60,000 years (main phase)

"The Great Dying" — the most devastating mass extinction in Earth's history. Massive flood basalt eruptions in Siberia released enough CO₂ and SO₂ to drive extreme global warming (8–10°C), ocean acidification, widespread ocean anoxia, and ozone destruction. Trilobites, rugose corals, fusulinid foraminifera, and most synapsids were wiped out. Recovery took over 10 million years — the longest of any mass extinction.

4. End-Triassic Extinction (~201 Ma)

Species Lost ~80% of species
Primary Cause Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) volcanism
Duration ~600,000 years

As Pangaea began to rift apart, massive volcanic eruptions along the opening Central Atlantic released huge quantities of CO₂ and SO₂. The resulting global warming, ocean acidification, and environmental instability eliminated many large amphibians, most non-dinosaurian archosaurs (crurotarsans), and many marine groups including conodonts. This extinction cleared the ecological stage for dinosaurs, which rose to dominance in the Jurassic.

5. End-Cretaceous (K-Pg) Extinction (~66 Ma)

Species Lost ~76% of species
Primary Cause Chicxulub asteroid impact (+ Deccan Traps volcanism)
Duration Geologically instantaneous (main kill within years)

The most famous mass extinction, caused by a 10–15 km asteroid striking the Yucatán Peninsula. The impact triggered global wildfires, an "impact winter" of darkness lasting months to years, acid rain, and extreme temperature swings. All non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, mosasaurs, ammonites, and many other groups went extinct. Small mammals, birds, crocodilians, turtles, and seed plants survived, inheriting a devastated world.

Patterns Across Extinctions

  • Volcanism is the leading cause — Three of the five extinctions (Permian, Triassic, and possibly Devonian) are linked to Large Igneous Province (LIP) eruptions. Massive volcanism destabilizes climate through CO₂ warming, SO₂ cooling, ocean acidification, and ozone destruction.
  • Ocean anoxia is the primary marine kill mechanism — Oxygen-depleted oceans appear in the geological record of all five events.
  • Recovery takes millions of years — After each extinction, it took 3–10 million years for biodiversity to approach pre-extinction levels.
  • Ecological restructuring follows — Every extinction eliminated dominant groups and allowed previously minor lineages to radiate. Without the Permian extinction, there would be no dinosaurs. Without the K-Pg extinction, there would be no human civilization.
  • Tropical species are most vulnerable — Organisms adapted to narrow temperature ranges in tropical environments suffer disproportionately during climate shifts.

The Sixth Extinction?

Many scientists argue that we are in the early stages of a sixth mass extinction, this time driven not by asteroids or volcanoes but by a single species: Homo sapiens. Current extinction rates are estimated at 100–1,000 times the normal background rate. Habitat destruction, climate change, pollution, invasive species, and overexploitation are eliminating species faster than at any time since the K-Pg event.

Did You Know?

Despite their devastation, mass extinctions have consistently been followed by bursts of evolutionary innovation. The Cambrian Explosion followed Snowball Earth. Dinosaurs rose after the Permian and Triassic extinctions. Mammals diversified after the K-Pg event. Destruction and creation are inseparably linked in the history of life.
