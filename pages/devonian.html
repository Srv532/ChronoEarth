The Devonian Period

419 – 359 million years ago

The Age of Fishes

The Devonian Period is known as the "Age of Fishes" for good reason — fish diversified into a staggering array of forms, from armored placoderms to the first sharks and bony fish. But the Devonian's significance extends far beyond the seas. It was during this period that the first forests appeared, transforming barren continents into green landscapes, and the first vertebrates crawled from water onto land — one of the most pivotal transitions in the history of life.

Masters of the Sea

Placoderms

Placoderms were armored jawed fish that dominated Devonian seas. The most fearsome was Dunkleosteus — a 6-to-8-meter apex predator with bony jaw plates capable of generating one of the most powerful bite forces in the history of life. Rather than teeth, Dunkleosteus had self-sharpening blades of bone that could slice through armored prey.

Sharks

The first true sharks appeared in the Devonian. Cladoselache, about 1.8 meters long, was a fast, streamlined predator from the Late Devonian seas of North America. Unlike modern sharks, it lacked scales on much of its body and could not open its mouth as wide. Sharks would go on to survive every subsequent mass extinction.

Lobe-Finned Fish

The sarcopterygians (lobe-finned fish) were perhaps the most consequential Devonian fish. Their fleshy, bony fins contained the same skeletal pattern — one bone, two bones, many bones — found in all tetrapod (four-limbed) vertebrate limbs. From this lineage would emerge the first land vertebrates.

From Water to Land

The transition from aquatic fish to terrestrial tetrapods is one of the best-documented evolutionary transitions in the fossil record. It did not happen in a single leap but through a series of intermediate forms:

  • Tiktaalik (~375 Ma) — Discovered in Arctic Canada in 2004, Tiktaalik is famously described as a "fishapod." It had fish-like scales and gills but also a flattened head, a mobile neck, and fin-bones that could support weight — adaptations for propping itself up in shallow water.
  • Acanthostega (~365 Ma) — One of the earliest known tetrapods, Acanthostega had eight digits on each limb but was still primarily aquatic. Its limbs were paddle-like, and it retained internal gills.
  • Ichthyostega (~365 Ma) — More robust than Acanthostega, with stronger limbs capable of supporting its body out of water. It likely hauled itself across mudflats like a large, awkward salamander.

These transitional organisms likely ventured onto land not to colonize it permanently, but to move between drying pools, escape aquatic predators, or exploit food sources like arthropods that had already colonized the shore.

The First Forests

The Devonian transformed Earth's landscapes from barren rock to lush forest. Early Devonian plants were small (knee-height), but by the Late Devonian, the first true trees had evolved:

  • Archaeopteris — The world's first large tree, reaching up to 30 meters tall. It had a woody trunk like a conifer but reproduced with spores like a fern. Archaeopteris forests covered vast areas of the Late Devonian landscape.
  • Wattieza — The oldest known tree, from the Gilboa Fossil Forest in New York State (~385 Ma). These tree-ferns stood about 8 meters tall.

The spread of forests had profound global consequences. Deep root systems accelerated rock weathering, drawing down atmospheric CO₂ and cooling the climate. Dead plant matter accumulated in soils and waterways, altering nutrient cycles in rivers and oceans.

The Late Devonian Extinction

The Late Devonian saw one of the "Big Five" mass extinctions — actually a prolonged crisis spanning roughly 20 million years with several extinction pulses, the most severe being the Kellwasser Event (~372 Ma) and the Hangenberg Event (~359 Ma). Together, these events eliminated an estimated 70–80% of species. Tropical marine communities, especially reef ecosystems, were devastated. Reef-building organisms were nearly wiped out, and complex reef ecosystems would not fully recover for 100 million years.

The causes remain debated. Leading hypotheses include ocean anoxia driven by nutrient runoff from newly forested continents, volcanic activity, and climate cooling from CO₂ drawdown by expanding forests.

Did You Know?

When Tiktaalik was discovered, paleontologists had predicted exactly where to find it. They needed Devonian-aged, shallow freshwater sedimentary rock that hadn't been extensively studied. They chose Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic — and found their "fishapod" on the very expedition, a stunning vindication of evolutionary prediction.
