The Permian Extinction

~252 million years ago (End of the Permian Period, 299–252 Ma)

The Great Dying

The End-Permian Extinction — known as "The Great Dying" — was the most catastrophic mass extinction in Earth's history. Approximately 96% of all marine species and 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species were wiped out in a geological instant — perhaps as little as 60,000 years. Life on Earth came closer to total annihilation than at any other time. It took over 10 million years for ecosystems to fully recover.

The Permian World Before the Extinction

The Permian Period (299–252 Ma) was shaped by the supercontinent Pangaea, which united all major landmasses into a single vast continent stretching from pole to pole. The interior of Pangaea was an immense desert — far from any ocean, it received almost no rainfall. Coastal regions supported diverse ecosystems, and the surrounding super-ocean, Panthalassa, covered more than 70% of Earth's surface.

  • Synapsids Ruled the Land — Before dinosaurs, the dominant terrestrial vertebrates were synapsids — the lineage that would eventually give rise to mammals. Gorgonopsids, therapsids, and dicynodonts filled predator and herbivore niches across Pangaea.
  • Rich Marine Life — Permian seas hosted diverse reef systems built by bryozoans, sponges, and rugose corals. Brachiopods, crinoids, and fusulinid foraminifera were abundant.
  • Diverse Insects — Insect diversity was high, including early beetles, primitive dragonflies, and the first insect species capable of folding their wings flat against their bodies.

The Cause: Siberian Traps

The primary trigger was one of the largest volcanic events in Earth's history: the eruption of the Siberian Traps. Over roughly 2 million years (peaking around 252 Ma), massive flood basalt eruptions covered an area of modern Siberia roughly equal to Western Europe — approximately 7 million km² — with lava up to 3 km thick in places.

The eruptions themselves were devastating, but the real killing mechanism was the gases they released:

  • Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) — Enormous volumes of CO₂ drove extreme global warming of 8–10°C. The magma also intruded through coal and carbonate deposits, releasing additional CO₂ and methane.
  • Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂) — Caused acid rain that devastated terrestrial vegetation and acidified surface ocean waters.
  • Hydrogen Fluoride and Chlorine — These halogen gases damaged the ozone layer, exposing land organisms to lethal ultraviolet radiation. Mutant spore fossils found in end-Permian sediments support this hypothesis.

The Cascade of Death

  1. Global Warming — Tropical ocean temperatures rose to 40°C — lethally hot for most marine organisms. Equatorial regions became uninhabitable "dead zones."
  2. Ocean Anoxia — Warmer oceans hold less dissolved oxygen. Stratification prevented circulation, and vast stretches of the ocean became completely oxygen-free (anoxic). Euxinia — toxic hydrogen sulfide in the water column — poisoned marine life.
  3. Ocean Acidification — Massive CO₂ absorption acidified seawater, dissolving the calcium carbonate shells and skeletons of reef organisms, brachiopods, and foraminifera.
  4. Terrestrial Collapse — Acid rain, UV radiation, extreme heat, and wildfire destroyed forests. With vegetation gone, soils eroded catastrophically, choking rivers and coastal waters with sediment.
  5. Methane Release — Warming may have destabilized seafloor methane clathrates, releasing additional greenhouse gas in a runaway feedback loop.

What Was Lost

  • Trilobites — After 300 million years on Earth, trilobites went entirely extinct.
  • Rugose and Tabulate Corals — The dominant reef builders of the Paleozoic were wiped out completely. Modern corals (scleractinians) evolved later to replace them.
  • Fusulinid Foraminifera — These large, complex single-celled organisms, which had been abundant globally, vanished entirely.
  • Most Synapsids — The dominant land vertebrates were nearly eliminated, opening ecological space for the archosaurs (including dinosaurs) that would dominate the Mesozoic.
  • Most Insects — The only known mass extinction of insects, with many major orders disappearing.

Did You Know?

The Permian Extinction was so severe that it took 10 million years for global biodiversity to recover to pre-extinction levels. For comparison, the dinosaur extinction 186 million years later was relatively mild — "only" 76% of species were lost, and recovery took about 3 million years.
