The Carboniferous Period

359 – 299 million years ago

The Age of Coal

The Carboniferous Period takes its name from carbon — specifically, the vast deposits of coal that formed during this time, which now power much of modern civilization. For 60 million years, enormous swamp forests covered the tropical lowlands, populated by insects of enormous size, the first reptiles, and amphibians that rivaled modern crocodiles. The Carboniferous was a world of extremes: oxygen levels soared to the highest in Earth's history, ice caps grew over the South Pole, and the supercontinent Pangaea began to assemble.

The Coal Forests

The defining feature of the Carboniferous was its immense tropical swamp forests. These forests were unlike anything before or since:

  • Lepidodendron — Giant lycopsid (club moss) trees reaching 40 meters tall with distinctive diamond-patterned bark. Despite their size, they were not true wood — their trunks were mostly bark surrounding a hollow core.
  • Sigillaria — Another giant lycopsid, similar to Lepidodendron but with vertically ribbed bark and a less-branched crown.
  • Calamites — Giant horsetails standing up to 20 meters tall, growing in dense thickets along waterways. Modern horsetails rarely exceed 1 meter.
  • Tree Ferns — True ferns with woody trunks up to 15 meters tall formed a lush understory beneath the lycopsid canopy.

When these trees died, they fell into the waterlogged swamps where acidic, oxygen-poor conditions prevented decomposition. Layer after layer of dead vegetation accumulated over millions of years, compressed by sediment into peat, then lignite, then coal. A critical factor: the fungi and bacteria capable of efficiently breaking down lignin (the tough structural polymer in wood) had not yet fully evolved, so dead wood accumulated far faster than it decayed.

The Age of Giant Insects

Atmospheric oxygen during the Late Carboniferous reached approximately 35% — compared to 21% today. This oxygen surplus enabled arthropods to grow to extraordinary sizes, because insects breathe through passive diffusion via tracheal tubes, and higher oxygen concentrations allow these tubes to supply larger bodies.

  • Meganeura — A dragonfly-like insect with a wingspan of up to 75 cm (2.5 feet), making it one of the largest flying insects ever to live. It was an aerial predator, hunting other insects on the wing.
  • Arthropleura — A giant millipede-like arthropod reaching 2.6 meters in length — the largest known land invertebrate in Earth's history. Despite its fearsome size, it was likely a herbivore or detritivore feeding on forest-floor litter.
  • Pulmonoscorpius — A scorpion reaching 70 cm long, prowling the coal forest floors.

The First Reptiles

The Carboniferous witnessed a breakthrough in vertebrate evolution: the amniotic egg. Amphibians must return to water to lay their jelly-like eggs, but the amniotic egg — with its protective shell, internal membranes, and self-contained fluid supply — freed reptiles from dependence on water for reproduction.

Hylonomus, discovered in fossil tree stumps at Joggins, Nova Scotia (~312 Ma), is one of the earliest known reptiles. This small, lizard-like insectivore was only about 20 cm long, but its evolutionary innovation — the amniotic egg — would enable its descendants to conquer every terrestrial habitat, eventually giving rise to dinosaurs, birds, and mammals.

Pangaea Begins to Form

During the Late Carboniferous, the continents Laurussia (North America + Europe) and Gondwana (South America + Africa + India + Antarctica + Australia) collided in a mountain-building event called the Variscan/Hercynian Orogeny. The resulting mountain chain stretched from modern Appalachia to the Urals. This collision was the first step in assembling Pangaea — the last great supercontinent — which would reach its full extent in the Permian.

The Late Paleozoic Ice Age

While the tropics sweltered under thick forests, the South Pole was buried under massive ice sheets. The Late Paleozoic Ice Age (also called the Karoo Ice Age) lasted from roughly 360 to 260 million years ago and was driven in part by the enormous carbon burial occurring in the coal swamps, which removed CO₂ from the atmosphere and reduced the greenhouse effect.

Did You Know?

The coal you burn today is literally fossilized Carboniferous sunlight. Those ancient swamp trees captured solar energy through photosynthesis 300 million years ago, locked it in carbon bonds, and preserved it underground. When we burn coal, we are releasing energy that last saw sunlight before dinosaurs existed.
