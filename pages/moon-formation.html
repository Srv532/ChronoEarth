Moon Formation

~4.5 billion years ago

The Giant Impact

Earth's Moon is not just a glowing orb in the night sky; it is the spectacular product of the most violent and catastrophic event in our planet's entire history. Approximately 4.5 billion years ago — a mere 30 to 50 million years after the Earth itself had first formed from the solar nebula — our young world experienced an apocalyptic collision. A Mars-sized protoplanet, widely known in the scientific community as Theia, slammed directly into the infant Earth at a glancing angle at speeds exceeding tens of thousands of kilometers per hour.

The resulting collision was so unbelievably energetic that it generated heat capable of partially melting both planetary bodies. The impact sheared off Earth's outer layers and entirely obliterated Theia, ejecting a vast, superheated cloud of vaporized rock, magma, and metallic debris into orbit around the crippled Earth. In the aftermath, over a remarkably short period—perhaps within just a few decades or a century—this swirling ring of orbiting material rapidly cooled and coalesced under the force of its own gravity to form the Moon we see today.

Theia: The Lost Planet

Theia is the name given to the hypothetical planetary embryo that collided with Earth, setting the stage for the Moon's creation. Fittingly named after the Greek Titan Theia, who was the mythological mother of Selene (the goddess of the Moon), this rogue protoplanet is believed to have formed in roughly the same orbital zone as Earth. State-of-the-art computer simulations and astrophysical models suggest that Theia was approximately the size of Mars — meaning it was roughly half the diameter of Earth and possessed about one-tenth of its total mass.

Tragically, Theia did not survive this apocalyptic encounter as an independent celestial body. During the cataclysmic impact, Theia was completely shattered. Its heavy, dense iron core plummeted into Earth and merged seamlessly with our own planet's core, significantly boosting Earth's mass and strengthening its magnetic field. Meanwhile, lighter silicate material from the mantles of both Earth and Theia was blasted violently into space. This dynamic exchange explains a fascinating lunar mystery: why the Moon possesses a proportionally tiny iron core compared to other rocky bodies in the solar system. The vast majority of Theia's heavy iron was simply swallowed by the Earth.

The Collision Sequence

  1. Impact (Hour 0) — Theia strikes Earth at roughly 4 km/s at an oblique angle of about 45°. The energy released is equivalent to billions of nuclear weapons. Both bodies are partially vaporized.
  2. Debris Disk (Hours to Days) — A superheated cloud of silicate vapor and molten droplets expands outward, forming a disk around the battered Earth. Temperatures in the disk exceed 4,000°C.
  3. Accretion (Weeks to Centuries) — Material in the disk beyond the Roche limit begins clumping together under its own gravity. The Moon assembles rapidly, possibly within 1 to 100 years.
  4. Lunar Magma Ocean (Millennia) — The newly formed Moon is entirely molten, covered by a global magma ocean hundreds of kilometers deep. As it cools, lighter minerals (plagioclase feldspar) float to form the bright lunar highlands we see today.

Evidence for the Giant Impact

  • Identical Oxygen Isotopes — Earth and the Moon share nearly identical oxygen isotope ratios, suggesting they formed from the same pool of material. Other solar system bodies have distinctly different ratios.
  • Iron-Poor Moon — The Moon's density is much lower than Earth's, and it has a tiny iron core (roughly 1–3% of its mass versus Earth's 32%). This matches the prediction that the Moon formed primarily from mantle material.
  • Angular Momentum — The Earth-Moon system has unusually high angular momentum, consistent with a large oblique impact.
  • Apollo Samples — Lunar rocks returned by the Apollo missions show evidence of an early global magma ocean, exactly as the Giant Impact Hypothesis predicts.

Why the Moon Matters

The violent formation of the Moon was not just an impressive celestial fireworks display; it had profound, long-lasting consequences that fundamentally shaped Earth's ultimate habitability and future. Without the Moon, the Earth as we know it simply would not exist:

  • Stabilized Axial Tilt — The Moon's immense gravitational pull acts as an anchor, stabilizing Earth's axial tilt at approximately 23.5 degrees. Without the Moon, Earth's tilt would wobble wildly over time, causing chaotic, extreme climate oscillations that could plunge the planet into deep freezes or scorching heatwaves, severely hindering the evolution of complex life.
  • Tidal Forces and the Origin of Life — The gravitational tug-of-war between Earth and the close-orbiting young Moon created massive tidal forces. These powerful tides churned the ancient oceans, dragging essential minerals from the land into the sea, and created dynamic tidal pools. Many scientists believe these fluctuating pools provided the perfect mixing bowl for early chemical reactions, potentially playing a crucial role in sparking the origin of life.
  • Slowed Earth's Rotation — In the immediate aftermath of the impact, Earth was spinning at a dizzying rate, completing a full day in just about 6 hours. Over billions of years, the steady drag of lunar tidal friction has acted as a planetary brake, gradually slowing Earth's rotation down to our current, comfortable 24-hour day. As it slows the Earth, the Moon gains angular momentum and continues to slowly drift away from us at a rate of about 3.8 centimeters per year.

Did You Know?

When the Moon first formed, it orbited just 22,500 km from Earth — roughly one-sixteenth of its current distance. It would have appeared about 15 times larger in the sky than it does today, and tides would have been thousands of times stronger.
