Ordovician & Silurian Periods

485 – 419 million years ago

The Ordovician Period (485 – 444 Ma)

The Ordovician was a time of incredible marine diversity. In what is sometimes called the "Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event" (GOBE), marine invertebrate families tripled in number, filling ecological niches across the world's shallow seas. Reef ecosystems flourished for the first time, built by corals, bryozoans, and stromatoporoids. The seas were ruled by nautiloids — giant squid-like cephalopods with straight or coiled shells, some reaching over 6 meters in length.

The Ordovician World

Gondwana, the southern supercontinent comprising modern Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia, and India, dominated the Southern Hemisphere. Smaller continents — Laurentia (North America), Baltica (Northern Europe), and Siberia — were scattered near the equator, surrounded by warm, shallow seas that supported extraordinary biodiversity. Sea levels were among the highest in Earth's history, flooding vast areas of continental interiors.

Life in the Ordovician Seas

  • Trilobites — Continued to diversify, with many new forms evolving elaborate spines and enrollment (curling) capabilities for defense.
  • Graptolites — Colonial planktonic organisms that drifted in vast numbers through Ordovician oceans. Their distinctive saw-blade fossils are key index fossils for dating Ordovician and Silurian rocks.
  • Brachiopods — Shelled marine organisms superficially resembling clams but unrelated. They were the dominant shellfish of the Paleozoic, carpeting seafloors in enormous numbers.
  • Crinoids — "Sea lilies" — echinoderms that lived attached to the seafloor on long stalks, filter-feeding with feathery arms. Their stem segments are among the most common Paleozoic fossils.
  • First Land Plants? — Spore-like microfossils from the Middle Ordovician suggest that the very first plants — simple, moss-like organisms — may have begun colonizing barren land surfaces with the help of symbiotic fungi.

The End-Ordovician Extinction

The Ordovician ended with one of the five great mass extinctions. Approximately 85% of marine species were wiped out in two pulses:

  1. First Pulse — Gondwana drifted over the South Pole, triggering massive glaciation. Sea levels plummeted by up to 100 meters, draining the shallow seas where most life lived. Cold-water species replaced warm-water communities.
  2. Second Pulse — As glaciers melted rapidly, sea levels rebounded, flooding continental shelves with anoxic (oxygen-depleted) deep water. Organisms that had survived the cold now suffocated in the warm, stagnant seas.

The Silurian Period (444 – 419 Ma)

The Silurian was a period of recovery and innovation following the devastation of the End-Ordovician extinction. Warm, stable climates returned. The ozone layer was well established, and for the first time, life made a sustained push onto dry land.

Silurian Milestones

Conquest of the Land

The earliest vascular land plants — plants with internal plumbing for transporting water and nutrients — appeared in the Silurian. Cooksonia, a tiny, forking stem just a few centimeters tall, is one of the oldest known vascular plants (~430 Ma). These pioneers lacked leaves and roots, but their vascular tissue allowed them to grow taller than the mosses and liverworts that preceded them.

Alongside the plants, the first terrestrial arthropods — millipede-like creatures and early arachnids — ventured onto land, beginning the colonization of a world that had been barren rock for 4 billion years.

Jawed Fish

The Silurian saw the evolution of gnathostomes — the first jawed vertebrates. Jaws evolved from the gill arches of jawless fish, and this innovation revolutionized vertebrate feeding. The earliest jawed fish were acanthodians ("spiny sharks") and placoderms (armored fish), both of which diversified dramatically in the following Devonian Period.

Coral Reef Expansion

Tabulate and rugose corals, along with stromatoporoids, built extensive reef systems in the warm Silurian seas. These reefs supported diverse communities of fish, crinoids, brachiopods, and trilobites — ecological structures comparable in complexity (though not in scale) to modern coral reefs.

Did You Know?

Before land plants evolved, Earth's continents were entirely barren — naked rock, sand, and dust, with no soil. The first plants had to build the soil they grew in, using their own decaying remains mixed with weathered rock. It took hundreds of millions of years to create the fertile soils we take for granted today.
